Grace’s story

Grace is 21 and in her third year of medical school in Manchester. She wanted to become a brain cancer doctor.

In the middle of a neurology lecture, she had a seizure and collapsed. An ambulance took her to Salford Royal Hospital. The scan showed a 4.2 cm tumour. A few days later, doctors confirmed it: glioblastoma, the most aggressive brain cancer there is.

Surgeons removed as much of it as they safely could, and the NHS covered her radiotherapy and chemotherapy. But Grace’s tumour has a genetic marker that makes standard chemotherapy work poorly. On that treatment alone, the cancer is expected to return in about five months.

There is a treatment that trains her own immune system to fight the tumour: a dendritic cell vaccine made from her blood at a clinic in Cologne. It has been approved for her, and she has a place in November. The NHS does not fund it, so the family is raising £90,000 to pay for it.

Why doesn’t the NHS cover this?

Covered by the NHS

  • Emergency scans and surgery
  • 30 sessions of radiotherapy
  • Standard chemotherapy

Not covered

  • Immune-cell vaccine in Germany
  • Proton therapy abroad
  • Travel and accommodation

The vaccine is made individually for each patient. The UK has no approval route for it yet, so no public health system in Europe pays for it.
